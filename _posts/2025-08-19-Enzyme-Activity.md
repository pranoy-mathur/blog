**Purpose:** To see how acid and heat affect enzyme activity, using pepsin on egg white and catalase on hydrogen peroxide.

**Principle:**
Enzymes are proteins that speed up reactions. A complete enzyme is made of an apoenzyme (the protein part) and a cofactor. Cofactors can be inorganic, or organic, in which case they are called prosthetic groups or coenzymes. The apoenzyme will not work without its cofactor.

The active site of an enzyme has two parts: the binding site, where the substrate interacts chemically with the enzyme, and the catalytic site, the small region where the reaction happens.

Enzymes are grouped into six classes by function: oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases and ligases (synthetases).

The main factors that affect enzyme activity are temperature, pH, substrate concentration and enzyme concentration.

Peptidases break peptide bonds. Exopeptidases can only snip amino acids from the ends of a chain, while endopeptidases can break peptide bonds at any point inside the chain. Pepsin is an endopeptidase found in the stomach. It works best in strongly acidic conditions (around pH 1.5 to 2), which is why HCl is added. <!-- added: pepsin's optimum pH --> Boiling denatures enzymes: the heat breaks the bonds that hold their shape, so the active site no longer fits the substrate. <!-- added: why boiling inactivates enzymes -->

Catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen, so active catalase produces bubbles. <!-- added: catalase reaction -->

**Materials:**
- Boiled egg
- Pepsin
- HCl
- Hydrogen peroxide
- Test tubes, pipettes and glass slides
- Heat source

**Protocol:**
*Pepsin*
1. I cut a boiled egg into equal-sized pieces and put one piece in each of four test tubes.
2. I set up the tubes as follows:

| Tube 1 | Tube 2 | Tube 3 | Tube 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 mL pepsin | 3 drops HCl | 1 mL pepsin + 3 drops HCl | 1 mL boiled pepsin + 3 drops HCl |

3. I compared how much of the egg was broken down in each tube.
