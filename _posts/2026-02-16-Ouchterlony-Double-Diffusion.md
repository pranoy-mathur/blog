**Purpose:** To use Ouchterlony double diffusion as a semi-quantitative way to study antigen-antibody reactions.

**Principle:**
An antigen is a substance that stimulates a specific immune response. The part of the antigen that an antibody binds to is the epitope, and the part of the antibody that binds the antigen is the paratope.

Not every antigen can start an immune response on its own:
- An **immunogen** can stimulate a specific immune response by itself. Immunogens usually have a high molecular weight, such as proteins, lipoproteins, polysaccharides and some nucleic acids.
- A **hapten** has a low molecular weight. It can bind to antibodies or T cells but cannot stimulate an immune response by itself. It becomes immunogenic when it is attached to a larger carrier molecule.

How strongly a substance triggers an immune response (its immunogenicity) depends on factors such as how foreign it is, its molecular size, its chemical composition and how easily it can be processed. <!-- from Lab Report doc -->

Antibodies are made of two heavy chains and two light chains held together by disulfide bonds. Different classes have different roles. For example, IgA is found in tears, IgE is involved in allergic reactions, and IgM is produced in the primary immune response. <!-- from Lab Report doc -->

In Ouchterlony double diffusion, wells are punched into an agarose gel. Antigens go into some wells and antiserum (antibodies) into another, and both diffuse outward through the gel toward each other. Where an antigen meets its matching antibody in the right proportions, they form a large insoluble complex that shows up as a white precipitin line. <!-- added: standard explanation of precipitin line formation --> The shape of the lines between neighboring antigen wells shows how the antigens are related:
- A smooth, joined arc means identity: the antigens share the same epitopes.
- An arc with a spur means partial identity: the antigens share some epitopes but not all.
- Two lines that cross means non-identity: the antigens share no epitopes.
<!-- added: standard interpretation patterns -->

**Protocol:**
<!-- protocol from Lab Report doc; the notebook only has the heading -->
1. I prepared 10 mL of 1% agarose (0.1 g of agarose) in 1x assay buffer.
2. I heated it until the agarose dissolved completely, let it cool to 55 to 60°C, and poured 5 mL onto a clean, grease-free glass slide.
3. I let the gel set and punched wells using the gel puncher.
4. I added 10 µL of antiserum and antigens to the corresponding wells.
5. I incubated the slide in a moist chamber at 37°C overnight.
