**Purpose:** To classify bacteria as Gram-positive or Gram-negative using Gram staining.

**Principle:**
Gram staining was introduced in 1884 by Hans Christian Gram. It is a differential stain, so it uses more than one dye to tell different types of bacteria apart. Staining methods fall into three groups:
- **Simple staining:** one dye.
- **Differential staining:** for example Gram staining and acid-fast staining. Acid-fast staining picks out bacteria with waxy cell walls, such as *Mycobacterium*. <!-- added: corrected; my notes linked acid-fast staining to spores, which is what endospore staining detects -->
- **Special staining:** for specific structures, such as capsule, endospore and flagella staining.

Gram staining depends on the composition of the cell wall. Both types of bacteria have a peptidoglycan layer. Peptidoglycan is a lattice made of two alternating amino sugars, N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM). Each NAM has a short chain of 4 to 5 amino acids attached, and crosslinks between these chains make the structure strong.
- **Gram-positive** bacteria have a thick peptidoglycan layer (about 20 to 80 nm). <!-- from Lab Report doc; the notebook diagram was covered -->
- **Gram-negative** bacteria have a thin peptidoglycan layer (about 2 to 7 nm) and an outer membrane that contains lipopolysaccharide (LPS).

LPS acts as an endotoxin, so only Gram-negative bacteria carry endotoxins. Endotoxins are part of the outer membrane and are released when the bacteria break apart, while exotoxins are secreted by the bacteria. <!-- added: corrected; my notes described endotoxins as produced inside the bacteria -->

How the stain works, step by step:
1. **Crystal violet:** in water, crystal violet splits into CV+ and Cl- ions. CV+ passes through the cell wall of both types and binds to the negatively charged parts of the wall, so all cells turn violet.
2. **Iodine:** iodine acts as a mordant. It forms a large crystal violet-iodine (CV-I) complex inside the cell.
3. **Decolorizing with ethanol:** in Gram-negative bacteria, ethanol dissolves the lipid outer membrane and leaves pores, so the CV-I complex washes out and the cells become colorless. In Gram-positive bacteria, the thick peptidoglycan layer holds on to the complex, so the cells stay violet.
4. **Safranin counterstain:** safranin stains the colorless Gram-negative cells pink.

So Gram-positive bacteria look violet and Gram-negative bacteria look pink. Bacteria can also be described by shape: cocci (round), bacilli (rods) and spirilla (spiral).

**Materials:**
- Glass slides
- Bunsen burner (lamp)
- Gram's crystal violet, Gram's iodine and Gram's safranin
- 95% ethanol
- Water
- Microscope

**Protocol:**
1. I placed a drop of bacterial culture on one side of a slide and made a smear with another slide.
2. I heat fixed the smear with the lamp and let it dry.
3. I flooded the smear with Gram's crystal violet and waited 1 minute.
4. I washed the dye off under water, then flooded the slide with Gram's iodine for 1 minute.
5. I washed the slide under running water.
6. I decolorized the smear with 95% ethanol for 5 to 10 seconds.
7. I rinsed the slide with water.
8. I flooded the slide with Gram's safranin for 1 minute.
9. I washed and dried the slide.
10. I observed it under the microscope at 10x, 45x and 100x.
