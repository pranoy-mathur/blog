**Purpose:** To test how well different antibiotics and plant extracts (phytochemicals) inhibit bacterial growth using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion test.

**Principle:**
An antibiotic is an antimicrobial substance that acts against bacteria. Antibiotics are produced naturally by soil microbes and are also made industrially.

An antibiotic sensitivity test (AST) is an in vitro test where a bacterial culture is exposed to antibiotics to see how susceptible it is. It is used to choose the best antibiotic for a specific case, to track changing resistance trends in local isolates, and to monitor antimicrobial resistance (AMR).

There are three types of AST:
1. **Diffusion (Kirby-Bauer test):** paper discs soaked in antibiotic are placed on an agar plate spread with bacteria. The antibiotic diffuses out into the agar. Where it stops the bacteria from growing, a clear zone of inhibition forms around the disc. A larger zone means the bacteria are more susceptible, and no zone means the bacteria are resistant. Mueller-Hinton agar (beef infusion, peptone and starch) is the standard medium for disc diffusion.
2. **Dilution (tube or agar dilution):** the drug is serially diluted, and the bacteria are exposed to each concentration, for example in a 96-well plate. This finds the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), the lowest concentration that stops visible growth, and the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), the lowest concentration that kills the bacteria. <!-- added: corrected definitions; my notes gave MIC as 50% killed and MBC as 90% -->
3. **Diffusion and dilution (E-test, or epsilometer test):** a strip with a gradient of antibiotic combines both principles.

*Phytochemicals*

Plants make primary metabolites, which take part directly in metabolism for growth and development, and secondary metabolites, which act as a first line of defense. Secondary metabolites are also called phytochemicals. Some have anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant (preventing oxidation that can lead to mutations), antibacterial and antiviral properties. The main types are saponins, terpenoids, polyphenols, flavonoids and alkaloids. Examples include curcumin from turmeric and resveratrol from grapes. Polyphenols dissolve in organic solvents that are less polar than water. I used plant extracts alongside a standard antibiotic to compare their effects.

**Protocol:**
<!-- protocol from Lab Report doc; it says LB agar was used instead of Mueller-Hinton -->
1. I cultured the bacteria in a test tube of MacConkey broth.
2. I poured a sample of the culture onto the center of an LB agar plate.
3. I sterilized a glass rod in the Bunsen burner flame.
4. I spread the sample evenly across the plate.
5. I soaked paper discs in each of the antibiotics and extracts.
6. I placed the discs on the plate, far apart from each other, sterilizing the tweezers before picking up each new disc.
7. I incubated the plate for 24 hours. <!-- doc says "inoculate"; corrected to incubate -->
