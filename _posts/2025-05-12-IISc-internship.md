In April and May 2025 I interned in the Department of Microbiology and Cell Biology at the Indian Institute of Science, in Prof. Dipshikha Chakravortty's lab, which works on *Salmonella* pathogenesis. I got the position by cold-emailing the lab.

I was assigned to a PhD student who taught me. Early on I mostly learned techniques and helped with other people's experiments. Later I ran a small experiment of my own, which I have written up [separately](/salmonella-growth-stress/).

**What the lab works on**

The lab studies how *Salmonella* causes disease. Each professor runs a group of PhD students, and each student has their own thesis within that area. The projects I saw up close were about what happens after *Salmonella* gets inside a host cell: how it survives in the vacuole it ends up in, what it does to lysosomes, and whether infection reaches the brain.

Two of the lab meetings were presentation days. The first was "Understanding the impact of *Salmonella* infection on neurodegeneration and organelle interaction disruption," which was about TFEB, a transcription factor that binds the CLEAR motif and controls lysosome activity. The second was "Understanding the modulation of host-cell metabolites in response to *Salmonella* infection."

I understood maybe half of the first presentation. That was the useful part. I wrote down everything I did not follow, looked it up that evening, and asked about it the next day.

**What I actually did**

The honest version of a first lab internship is that a lot of it is preparation. I autoclaved and stacked plates, filled tip boxes, made PBS buffer and LB broth, prepared SS agar and MH agar, poured plates in the UV chamber, and disposed of used media. None of that is glamorous, but it is most of what keeps a lab running, and doing it badly ruins someone else's week.

The techniques I learned hands-on:

- **Counting bacterial colonies.** My first task was counting *Salmonella* colonies by hand for a Master's student studying proliferation on epithelial lining.
- **Flow cytometry.** I used this more than anything else. Propidium iodide for live and dead cells, since it only crosses the membrane of dead cells and binds their DNA. BCECF for intracellular acidification, which happens under stress such as antibiotic pressure. DCFDA for reactive oxygen species, since it is non-fluorescent until oxidized inside the cell. I also ran membrane potential on the machine for a PhD student.
- **Sterilization, media preparation and plating.** Autoclaving at 121 °C and 15 psi, UV for the plates, spreading with an L-shaped rod sterilized in alcohol, and streaking.
- **RNA isolation by the Trizol method**, twice, including homogenizing tissue with glass beads.
- **Agarose gel electrophoresis**, including running a gel for cDNA.
- **Nanodrop spectrophotometry** to check the purity and concentration of RNA, and DNase treatment to clean up the samples.
- **cDNA synthesis and qPCR.** We ran 39 cycles with SYBR Green, then a melt curve to check that only one product had been amplified.
- **Plasmid isolation by alkaline lysis.**
- **Optical density** on a 96-well plate.
- **Image analysis** in XEI software, measuring the length and height of individual *Salmonella* cells from atomic force microscope images.

I have written up several of these techniques in detail in my [lab notebook series](/lab-notebook/), from a later internship.

**Things worth writing down**

A few explanations stuck with me more than the protocols did.

SS agar tells three food-borne pathogens apart on one plate, purely through their metabolism. *E. coli* ferments lactose into lactic acid, which turns an indicator in the agar, so its colonies come up pink. *Salmonella* does not ferment lactose, but it has thiosulfate reductase, which converts sodium thiosulfate in the agar to a black iron compound, so its colonies come up black. *Shigella* does neither, so its colonies stay colorless. <!-- notebook says ferrous sulphate; the black precipitate in SS agar is ferrous sulfide -->

The reason RNA work is so fussy is that RNase is on everything, including us. You are told not to talk or breathe over an open tube, and RNA goes on ice or in a cooled centrifuge because it falls apart so easily. The guanidinium isothiocyanate in Trizol is there to denature RNase before it can do that.

A melt curve works because every copy of the same product has the same number of hydrogen bonds, so they all come apart at the same temperature. Raise the temperature slowly and the fluorescence drops off a cliff at that point, which shows up as one peak. Two peaks means you amplified something you did not want.

**What I took away**

The internship confirmed that I want to work in biology. It also showed me that I am more drawn to applied and industry-facing work than to pure academic research, which is useful to find out early.

The other takeaway is less about the science. I spent a lot of it not understanding things, and the only way through was to write down the question and come back to it. The notebook I kept is mostly a list of things I did not know yet.
